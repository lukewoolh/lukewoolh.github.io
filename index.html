How to revise so it sticks

These six strategies come from research on how memory works. Each one is built into a part of this page.

Retrieval practice

Test yourself before you reread

Recalling information strengthens the memory far more than reading it again.

Here: the brain-dump box at the top of every topic, the quiz and the flashcards.

Spaced practice

Little and often

Four 20-minute sessions across a week beat one 80-minute cram the night before.

Here: the flashcards bring back the ones you find hard more often. Use the plan below.

Interleaving

Mix the topics

Switching between topics trains you to work out which idea a question needs, exactly like the exam.

Here: the quiz shuffles every topic together by default.

Elaboration

Ask "why?" and "how?"

Linking a fact to a reason builds a web of connections, so one clue unlocks the rest.

Here: the yellow "Ask yourself" questions in each topic.

Dual coding

Words plus pictures

Turn notes into tables, diagrams and timelines. Two kinds of memory are better than one.

Here: the packaging table, celebration grid and product development scale. Redraw them from memory.

Concrete examples

Every idea needs an example

Markers reward examples. "Glass protects food" is weak. "A glass jar keeps jam visible and can be hot-filled" is strong.

Here: the short-answer model answers show what a full-mark example looks like.

Know your command verbs

The verb tells you how much to write. Say what each one means out loud, then tap the card to check.

Mark rule of thumb: one well-explained point with an example is usually worth about 1 mark in an outline question and 2 marks in a discuss or explain question.

Question 21 · 15 marks · about 30 minutes

When planning a menu for a special occasion it is important to make considerations to ensure success of the event and to ensure the safety and comfort of the guests attending.

Explain how THREE menu planning considerations for special occasions can determine the success of an event.

Step 1 · Decode the question

  • Explain means relate cause and effect. For each consideration, say how and why it makes the event succeed or fail.
  • THREE considerations. Write one paragraph for each. Writing about five thin ones scores lower than three deep ones.
  • Keep coming back to success, safety and comfort of guests. These words are your link-back.

Step 2 · Choose your three considerations

Step 3 · Plan each paragraph with TEEL

T

Topic sentence. Name the consideration and state that it affects the success of the event.

E

Explain. How and why does it affect the event? Use cause-and-effect linking words.

E

Example. A specific dish, guest or situation at a specific occasion.

L

Link. Return to success, safety or guest comfort.

Cause-and-effect words: as a result · therefore · this means that · consequently · which leads to · because · so that · if… then…

Step 4 · Read a model paragraph

Model paragraph · health status of guests

[T] The health status and dietary needs of guests is a key menu planning consideration because it directly affects guest safety and comfort. [E] If a host does not find out about allergies, intolerances or medical conditions before planning, a guest could be served a food that causes a serious reaction. As a result, the event could end with a medical emergency rather than a celebration. Planning for these needs also means every guest has something they can enjoy, so no one feels left out. [E] For example, at a 16th birthday party where one guest has a peanut allergy and another has coeliac disease, the host could replace a satay chicken skewer with a lemon and herb version, choose a flourless chocolate cake, and label dishes clearly so guests can choose safely. Separate utensils would prevent cross-contamination. [L] Therefore, considering guests' health status makes the event both safe and inclusive, which is essential for it to be a success.
